What are the significant features of a maintained configuration system for this project?

A Configuration Management (CM) system for a nationwide EHR must ensure data integrity and version control across multiple teams. Key features include:

  • Version Control & Baselining: Tracking every change to the source code and establishing "baselines" (stable versions) for deployment across different clinics.
  • Change Control Board (CCB) Workflow: A formal process for approving changes to ensure that a modification in Team B’s billing module doesn’t break Team A’s patient records.

Why software planning tasks are considered inelastic? Remedies?

Software project planning tasks are considered inelastic because of the "interconnectedness" of tasks; you cannot simply compress the time required for logical design or testing without compromising quality.

  • Why they are inelastic: Large projects have fixed sequences. For example, Team B cannot analyze data (Analytics) until Team A has successfully built the data collection modules (Patient Management).
  • Remedies: * Scope Management: Reducing non-essential features to meet the deadline.
  • Parallelism: Overlapping tasks where possible (e.g., Team B starts designing schemas while Team A is still coding).
  • Incremental Delivery: Releasing the system in phases (e.g., Patient Management first, Billing later).
If Team A finishes earlier than Team B, what happens?

If Team A completes its components faster than Team B:

  • Bottlenecks: Team B becomes the "critical path." Even though Team A is done, the nationwide system cannot "go live" because patients can't be billed.
  • Idle Resources: Team A may face "downtime" or must be reassigned, which can lead to integration issues later if they are not available for bug fixing.
  • Integration Risk: If Team A moves on to other projects, the final integration testing with Team B’s components might be delayed or poorly executed due to a lack of original developers.
(e) The Mythical Man-Month principle

Fred Brooks’ law states: "Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later."

Application to this scenario: If the EHR project is delayed, bringing in new developers to help Team B with "Analytics" will likely cause further delays because:

  • Ramp-up Time: Existing Team B members must stop coding to train the new people on the complex healthcare logic.
  • Communication Overhead: As the number of people (nnn) increases, the communication channels increase exponentially by n(n−1)2\frac{n(n-1)}{2}2n(n−1)​, leading to more meetings and less coding.
  • Task Divisibility: Complex tasks like "System Analytics" are often not easily divisible; you cannot have two people write the same piece of logic simultaneously to finish twice as fast.

Question 06: Software Project Management Basics​

(a) What is software project management? Why is software project management important?

Software Project Management (SPM) is a proper way of planning, monitoring, and controlling software projects. It involves the use of knowledge, skills, tools, and techniques to meet project requirements within specific constraints like time, budget, and quality.

Importance of SPM

SPM is crucial because software development is an abstract process with high complexity. Its importance includes:

  • Managing Scope: Prevents "scope creep" by ensuring only agreed-upon features are developed.
  • Resource Optimization: Ensures that developers, testers, and infrastructure are used efficiently.
  • Risk Mitigation: Identifies potential bottlenecks (like technical debt or staff turnover) before they derail the project.
  • Quality Assurance: Ensures the final product meets the client's standards and functions reliably.
  • Cost & Time Control: Keeps the project within the financial budget and ensures timely delivery to the market.
(b) What key responsibilities will you have as a software project manager to ensure the project's success (E-commerce case study)?

Given the tight deadlines and evolving requirements of the e-commerce platform, your key responsibilities include:

  1. Defining the Project Lifecycle: Choosing an Agile/Scrum methodology to handle "evolving requirements" through iterative sprints.
  2. Stakeholder Management: Acting as a bridge between the retail client, the development team, and end-users to manage expectations and gather feedback.
  3. Task Scheduling & Prioritization: Using tools (like Jira or Gantt charts) to ensure high-priority features (like the payment gateway) are built first.
  4. Risk Management: Identifying risks early—such as security vulnerabilities in the payment system—and creating contingency plans.
  5. Team Leadership & Motivation: Clearing "blockers" for the developers to maintain high velocity under tight deadlines.
(c) Why are discounted cash flow (DCF) techniques considered more effective for project selection than using net profit or ROI?

Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) is superior because it accounts for the Time Value of Money (TVM)—the principle that a dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow.

FeatureNet Profit / ROIDiscounted Cash Flow (DCF)
Time ValueIgnores when the money is earned.Accounts for the timing of cash flows.

Summary: While ROI tells you how much you make, DCF tells you what that future profit is actually worth in today’s terms, allowing for a more "apples-to-apples" comparison between different projects.

Question 7: Project Failure and Estimation​

(a) Why do software development projects fail? Explain with proper examples.

Software projects often fail due to a combination of technical and managerial issues:

  1. Poor Requirement Analysis: Requirements are often vague or misunderstood.
  • Example: A client asks for a "search bar," but the developers build a simple text filter that can't handle the database load of 1 million products.
  1. Unrealistic Deadlines: Pressure from stakeholders leads to "death marches."
  • Example: Forcing a 6-month project into a 2-month window, resulting in buggy, unmaintainable code.
  1. Lack of User Involvement: Building a product that the end-user doesn't actually find useful.
  2. Technical Debt: Using outdated technology or rushing code, leading to a system that eventually crashes or cannot be updated.
(b) What remedial measures can be taken so that software development projects do not fail?

To prevent failure, project managers should implement:

  • Iterative Development (Agile): Deliver small pieces of working software frequently to get immediate feedback.
  • Rigorous Testing: Implement Automated Testing and Continuous Integration (CI/CD) to catch bugs early.
  • Clear Documentation: Ensure requirements and system architecture are well-documented.
  • Effective Communication: Hold daily stand-ups to identify and solve problems immediately.
  • Buffer Management: Always include a "buffer" in the schedule to account for unforeseen technical challenges.
(c) Describe the COCOMO technique for deriving effort and cost estimates for software projects.

The Constructive Cost Model (COCOMO) is an algorithmic model used to estimate the cost, effort, and schedule of a project based on the size of the software.

1. Project Types

COCOMO categorizes projects into three types:

  • Organic: Small teams, familiar environment (e.g., a simple payroll system).
  • Semi-Detached: Intermediate complexity and team size (e.g., a new database system).
  • Embedded: High complexity, tight constraints (e.g., air traffic control software).

2. The Levels

  • Basic COCOMO: Uses a simple formula based on Lines of Code (KLOC).
  • Intermediate COCOMO: Applies "Cost Drivers" (Product reliability, hardware constraints, personnel capability).
  • Detailed COCOMO: Accounts for the influence of individual project phases (analysis, design, etc.).

3. The Formula (Basic)

The effort EEE is typically calculated as:

E=a⋅(KLOC)bE = a \cdot (KLOC)^bE=a⋅(KLOC)b

Where a and b are constants based on the project type.

    • Identifies project activities and their dependencies.
    • Shows the sequence of tasks.

  4. Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)

    • Breaks the project into manageable tasks and sub-tasks.

  5. Resource Plan

    • Identifies the people, equipment, software, and other resources required.

  6. Effort and Cost Estimates

    • Estimates the effort, duration, and financial cost of each activity.

  7. Project Schedule

    • Specifies task start dates, finish dates, milestones, and deadlines.
    • Often represented using Gantt Charts or Network Diagrams.

  8. Risk Management Plan

    • Identifies project risks.
    • Includes mitigation and contingency plans.

  9. Project Budget

    • Summarizes the estimated project costs.

  10. Baseline Project Plan

  • The final approved project plan used to monitor and control project progress.
(b)(I) A software package is to be designed and built to assist in software cost estimation. It has been suggested that a software prototype would be valuable in these circumstances. Explain why this might be.

A prototype is an early working version of the software that demonstrates key features before full development.

Why a prototype is valuable

  1. Clarifies User Requirements

    • Users often find it difficult to specify all requirements initially.
    • A prototype allows them to interact with the system and identify missing or incorrect requirements.

  2. Improves Communication

    • Developers and stakeholders can discuss the system using a working model instead of abstract documents.

  3. Validates Cost Estimation Methods

    • Since the software performs cost estimation, the prototype allows users to verify whether estimation models and calculations are appropriate.

  4. Identifies Design Problems Early

    • Errors in user interface, workflow, or functionality can be discovered before full implementation.

  5. Reduces Development Risk

    • Technical challenges are identified early, reducing the likelihood of costly redesign later.

  6. Supports Better Decision Making

    • Stakeholders can evaluate whether the proposed system meets business needs before investing in full development.

  7. Increases User Satisfaction

    • Continuous user feedback results in software that better meets customer expectations.
(b)(II) Discuss how such prototyping could be controlled to ensure that it is conducted in an orderly and effective way and within a specified time span.

To prevent prototyping from becoming uncontrolled or delaying the project, the following controls should be applied:

  1. Define Clear Objectives
  • Specify exactly what the prototype should demonstrate.
  • Focus only on critical functions.
  1. Time-box the Prototype
  • Allocate a fixed development period (e.g., 2–4 weeks).
  • Stop prototype development when the time limit is reached.
  1. Limit the Scope
  • Include only essential features.
  • Avoid adding unnecessary functionality (scope creep).
  1. Involve Users Regularly
  • Schedule periodic review meetings.
  • Collect structured feedback after each iteration.
  1. Document Feedback
  • Record all suggested changes.
  • Prioritize them based on importance.
  1. Version Control
  • Maintain different prototype versions.
  • Track changes systematically.
  1. Establish Acceptance Criteria

  • Define what must be achieved before ending the prototyping phase.

  • Examples:

    • User interface approved.
    • Estimation process validated.
    • Key requirements confirmed.
  1. Management Review
  • Project manager monitors progress.
  • Ensure the prototype remains aligned with project objectives and schedule.
  1. Decide Whether to Discard or Evolve the Prototype

  • Determine in advance whether the prototype is:

    • Throwaway Prototype: Built only for learning and discarded.
    • Evolutionary Prototype: Gradually improved into the final system.
  1. Monitor Time and Cost
  • Compare actual effort against the planned budget.
  • Prevent excessive spending on prototype development.
8(c) What are the limitations of the precedence and CPM activity network notations?

Precedence Networks and Critical Path Method (CPM) are widely used for project scheduling, but they have several limitations.

  1. Assumes Fixed Activity Durations
  • CPM assumes activity durations are known with certainty.
  • Real software projects often involve uncertainty.
  1. Ignores Resource Constraints
  • Network diagrams focus on task dependencies.
  • They do not consider resource availability or conflicts.
  1. Large Networks Become Complex
  • Large projects may contain hundreds of activities.
  • The diagrams become difficult to understand and maintain.
  1. Frequent Updates Required
  • Changes in project activities require updating the entire network.
  • Maintaining the network can become time-consuming.
  1. Limited Representation of Uncertainty
  • CPM does not model uncertainty or risk.
  • It provides only one estimated completion time.
  1. Critical Path May Change
  • Delays in non-critical activities can create a new critical path.
  • Continuous monitoring is required.
  1. Difficult to Represent Iterative Development
  • Software projects often involve repeated development cycles.
  • Network diagrams assume a mostly sequential workflow.
  1. Does Not Show Resource Utilization
  • Cannot indicate whether resources are overloaded or underutilized.
  • Additional tools such as resource histograms are required.
  1. May Oversimplify Dependencies
  • Complex relationships between tasks are difficult to represent accurately.
  1. Requires Accurate Initial Planning
  • Incorrect estimates or missing activities reduce the usefulness of the network.

Question 08​

(a)(i) Why would a software prototype be valuable?

A software prototype is useful because it:

  • Clarifies user requirements.
  • Demonstrates system functionality before full development.
  • Identifies errors early.
  • Improves communication between developers and users.
  • Reduces development risk.
  • Allows users to provide early feedback.
  • Improves user satisfaction.
  • Reduces the cost of correcting mistakes later.
(a)(ii) How can prototyping be controlled?

To ensure prototyping is effective:

  • Clearly define the prototype's objectives.
  • Set a fixed development schedule.
  • Limit the scope of the prototype.
  • Identify which features will be included.
  • Review the prototype regularly with stakeholders.
  • Record all user feedback.
  • Avoid continuous changes without approval.
  • Stop prototyping once requirements are understood.
  • Convert approved requirements into the final system specification.
(b) At which stage would a prototype be useful?

(i) Telephone Sales System

Stage: Requirements Analysis

Reason:

  • Helps users visualize the interface.
  • Clarifies user requirements.
  • Improves communication between users and developers.
  • Identifies missing or incorrect requirements early.

(ii) Microsoft Access Performance Concerns

Stage: Feasibility Study (Technical Feasibility)

Reason:

  • Tests whether Microsoft Access can meet performance requirements.
  • Measures response times.
  • Evaluates system capacity.
  • Determines whether the proposed technology is suitable before development begins.

Buffer Locations

According to Critical Chain Project Management (CCPM):

  1. Project Buffer

Place a Project Buffer after the final activity H and before the project end.

A → E → F → H → Project Buffer → Finish
  1. Feeding Buffers

Buffers are inserted where non-critical paths join the critical chain.

  • Before H from C
  • Before H from G

Critical chain:

A + E + F + H

Safety removed:

  • E = 12 − 8 = 4

Start Activities as Late as Possible

Under Critical Chain scheduling:

  • Schedule all activities at their Latest Start (LS) times instead of their earliest start.
  • Keep safety time in the project and feeding buffers rather than within individual tasks.
  • This minimizes multitasking and protects the project completion date.
