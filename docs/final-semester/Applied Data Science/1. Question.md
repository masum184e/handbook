To find the most likely sequence for the observations **Jogging ($O_1$), Shopping ($O_2$), Stays Indoors ($O_3$)**, we use the **Viterbi Algorithm**.

**Day 1: Observation = Jogging**

We calculate $v_1(k) = P(O_1|k) \cdot P(k)$:

- **Sunny:** $0.7 \times 0.333 = 0.2331$

- **Cloudy:** $0.2 \times 0.333 = 0.0666$

**Day 2: Observation = Shopping**

- **Sunny:** $0.2 \times \max(0.2331 \cdot 0.6, 0.0333 \cdot 0.2, 0.0666 \cdot 0.3) = 0.2 \times 0.13986 = \mathbf{0.02797}$ (from Sunny)

**Day 3: Observation = Stays Indoors**

We calculate $v_3(k) = P(O_3|k) \cdot \max_{j}(v_2(j) \cdot P(k|j))$:

Entropy measures the "impurity" or randomness in the target variable (**Buy**).
The formula for Entropy is:

$$E(S) = \sum_{i=1}^{c} -p_i \log_2(p_i)$$

In our dataset of 6 samples:

- **Total Samples ($n$):** 6
- **Buy = Yes:** 3 (IDs 2, 3, 4)
- **Buy = No:** 3 (IDs 1, 5, 6)

Since the split is exactly $50/50$, the entropy will be 1:

$$E(S) = -\left(\frac{3}{6}\right) \log_2 \left(\frac{3}{6}\right) - \left(\frac{3}{6}\right) \log_2 \left(\frac{3}{6}\right)$$

$$E(S) = -0.5(-1) - 0.5(-1) = \mathbf{1.0}$$

<summary>(c) Which evaluation strategy should be used?</summary>


**Use k-Fold Cross-Validation**

A common choice is **k-Fold Cross-Validation (e.g., 5-fold or 10-fold)**.

Procedure:

1. Split the dataset into **k equal parts**.
2. Train on **k−1 folds**.
3. Test on the remaining fold.
4. Repeat until every fold has been used as the test set.
5. Compute the average performance.

**Why use Cross-Validation?**

It:

* Uses all data for both training and testing.
* Provides a reliable estimate of model performance.
* Detects overfitting and underfitting.
* Helps compare all three models fairly.

**Evaluation Metrics**

Use regression metrics such as:

* Mean Squared Error (MSE)
* Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE)
* Mean Absolute Error (MAE)
* (R^2) Score

Choose the model with:

* Lowest RMSE/MAE
* Highest (R^2)
* Small difference between training and validation performance

Use **k-Fold Cross-Validation (5-fold or 10-fold)** with regression metrics such as **RMSE, MAE, MSE, and (R^2)**. This strategy provides a reliable estimate of performance, helps detect overfitting, and ensures a good **bias–variance trade-off** by comparing the average validation performance of all three models.

<summary>(b) Determine the values of $a$ and $b$ for smallest IG, and $x$ and $y$ for largest IG.</summary>

**Information Gain (IG)** is a measure used in decision tree algorithms (such as ID3 and C4.5) to determine the best attribute for splitting a node. It measures the reduction in uncertainty (entropy) after a split.

The formula for Information Gain is:

$$
\boxed{\text{IG}=\text{Entropy(Parent)}-\text{Weighted Entropy(Children)}}
$$

Where:

* **Entropy(Parent)** is the impurity before splitting.
* **Weighted Entropy(Children)** is the combined impurity of the child nodes after splitting.

A good split **reduces entropy**, resulting in a **high Information Gain**.

**Smallest Information Gain**

The **smallest possible Information Gain is 0**.

This occurs when the split **does not reduce entropy**. In other words:

* The child nodes have the **same class distribution** as the parent node.
* The weighted entropy of the child nodes is equal to the entropy of the parent node.

Mathematically,

$$
\text{Entropy(Parent)}=\text{Weighted Entropy(Children)}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{\text{IG}=0}
$$

**Applying this to the given tree**

The left parent node contains

$$
(12,4)
$$

The class ratio is

$$
12:4=3:1
$$

To keep the entropy unchanged, the child nodes must also have the same ratio (3:1).

Choose

$$
\boxed{a=9,\qquad b=3}
$$

The remaining child becomes

$$
(12-9,4-3)=(3,1)
$$

Both child nodes have the ratio

$$
9:3=3:1
$$

and

$$
3:1=3:1
$$

Since both children have the same class distribution as the parent, the entropy does not change.

Hence,

$$
\boxed{a=9,b=3}
$$

gives the **smallest Information Gain (IG = 0)**.

**Largest Information Gain**

The **largest Information Gain** occurs when the split completely removes uncertainty.

This happens when:

* Each child node is **pure**, meaning it contains instances of only one class.
* The entropy of each child node is **0**.

Thus,

$$
\text{Weighted Entropy(Children)}=0
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{\text{IG}=\text{Entropy(Parent)}}
$$

which is the **maximum possible Information Gain**.

**Applying this to the given tree**

The right parent node contains

$$
(4,12)
$$

* Put all positive examples into one child.

The two child nodes are:

* First child: **(4,0)** → only positive examples (pure)
* Second child: **(0,12)** → only negative examples (pure)

Since both child nodes have zero entropy, the weighted entropy after splitting is zero, producing the **maximum Information Gain**.

Thus,

$$

$$

<summary>(c) Discuss three potential ways for reducing overfitting in a decision tree.</summary>

Overfitting in a decision tree occurs when the tree learns the training data too closely, including noise and outliers, which reduces its performance on new, unseen data. Three common ways to reduce overfitting are:

1. **Pruning the Tree**
   * Pruning removes branches that contribute little to improving prediction accuracy.
   * **Pre-pruning (Early Stopping):** Stops the tree from growing beyond a certain point by setting limits such as maximum depth or minimum samples required to split a node.
   * **Post-pruning:** Allows the tree to grow fully and then removes unnecessary branches based on validation performance.
   * **Benefit:** Produces a simpler model that generalizes better.
2. **Limiting Tree Depth**
   * Restrict the maximum depth of the decision tree.
   * A very deep tree tends to memorize the training data, while a shallower tree captures only the most important patterns.
   * **Benefit:** Reduces model complexity and lowers the risk of fitting noise in the data.
3. **Setting Minimum Samples for Splitting or Leaf Nodes**
   * Specify a minimum number of samples required to split an internal node or to remain in a leaf node.
   * This prevents the tree from creating branches based on very few observations, which are often caused by random variations.
   * **Benefit:** Leads to more reliable splits and improves the model's ability to generalize.

These techniques **pruning**, **limiting tree depth**, and **requiring a minimum number of samples for splits or leaf nodes** help create simpler decision trees that are less likely to overfit and more likely to perform well on unseen data.

### Question 07: Random Forest

<details>

<summary>(a) General principle of an Ensemble Method. What are Bagging and Boosting?</summary>

**General Principle of Ensemble Method**

An **ensemble method** is a machine learning technique that combines the predictions of **multiple models (called weak learners or base learners)** to produce a better overall prediction than any single model.

**Principle:**

* Train several models instead of one.
* Combine their outputs.
* The combined model has **higher accuracy, better stability, and lower error**.

**Advantages:**

* Improves prediction accuracy.
* Reduces overfitting.
* Increases robustness.
* Handles complex data better.

**Bagging (Bootstrap Aggregating)**

Bagging is an ensemble technique in which multiple models are trained **independently** on different bootstrap (randomly sampled with replacement) datasets, and their predictions are combined.

**Working:**

1. Draw several bootstrap samples from the training data.
2. Train one model (usually a decision tree) on each sample.
3. Combine predictions:
   * **Classification:** Majority voting.
   * **Regression:** Average of predictions.

**Characteristics:**

* Models are trained **in parallel**.
* Reduces **variance**.
* Prevents overfitting.
* Example: **Random Forest**.

**Boosting**

Boosting is an ensemble technique where models are trained **sequentially**, and each new model focuses more on the mistakes made by the previous model.

**Working:**

1. Train the first weak learner.
2. Increase the weight of incorrectly classified samples.
3. Train the next learner on the updated data.
4. Repeat the process.
5. Combine all learners using weighted voting.

**Characteristics:**

* Models are trained **one after another**.
* Reduces **bias**.
* Produces a strong learner from many weak learners.
* Examples:
  * AdaBoost
  * Gradient Boosting
  * XGBoost

**Difference between Bagging and Boosting**

| Bagging                      | Boosting                    |
| ---------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Models trained independently | Models trained sequentially |
| Uses bootstrap sampling      | Uses weighted data          |
| Reduces variance             | Reduces bias                |
| Parallel training            | Sequential training         |
| Example: Random Forest       | Example: AdaBoost, XGBoost  |

</details>

The **number of trees** is an important hyperparameter in Random Forest.

**Case 1: Too Few Trees**

* High variance.
* Predictions are unstable.
* Lower accuracy.
* Model depends heavily on a few trees.

**Case 2: Increasing the Number of Trees**

As the number of trees increases:

* Accuracy generally improves.
* Variance decreases.
* Predictions become more stable.
* Generalization improves.
* Overfitting usually does **not** increase significantly.

**Very Large Number of Trees**

Advantages:

* Stable predictions.
* Very low variance.

### Question 09: K-Means Clustering

<details>

<summary>(a) Steps of K-Means Clustering Algorithm</summary>


The **K-Means algorithm** partitions a dataset into **K clusters** such that each data point belongs to the cluster with the nearest centroid.

1. Choose the Number of Clusters (K): Decide the number of clusters (K).
2. Initialize Centroids: Randomly select (K) initial centroids (cluster centers).
3. Assign Data Points
    * Calculate the distance (usually Euclidean distance) from each data point to every centroid.
    * Assign each point to the **nearest centroid**.
4. Update Centroids
    * For each cluster, compute the new centroid as the **mean of all points** in that cluster.

$$
\text{Centroid}=\frac{\text{Sum of points in cluster}}{\text{Number of points}}
$$

5. Repeat
    * Repeat **Steps 3 and 4** until:
      * No point changes its cluster, or
      * The centroids do not change.
6. Output: The final clusters and their centroids.

<summary>(b) Accuracy is a good performance metric for imbalanced dataset - Justify your answer</summary>

**No, accuracy is generally *not* a good performance metric for an imbalanced dataset.**

In an **imbalanced dataset**, one class (majority class) has significantly more samples than the other (minority class). A model can achieve very high accuracy by simply predicting the majority class all the time, even though it completely fails to identify the minority class.

**Example:**

* Dataset: 1,000 samples
  * 990 are **Negative**
  * 10 are **Positive**
* A classifier predicts **all samples as Negative**.

Then:

* Correct predictions = 990
* Accuracy = **990/1000 = 99%**

Although the accuracy is **99%**, the model fails to detect any positive cases, making it ineffective for many real-world applications such as fraud detection or disease diagnosis.

**Better metrics for imbalanced datasets:**

* **Precision** – Measures how many predicted positives are actually positive.
* **Recall (Sensitivity)** – Measures how many actual positives are correctly identified.
* **F1-score** – Balances precision and recall.
* **ROC-AUC** or **PR-AUC** – Evaluate model performance across different thresholds.

Accuracy can be misleading for imbalanced datasets because it is dominated by the majority class. Metrics such as **precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC** provide a more reliable evaluation of model performance.

### Question 05: Confusion Matrix + ROC Curve

<details>

<summary>(a) what do you understand by underfitting and overfitting</summary>

**Underfitting** occurs when a machine learning model is **too simple** to capture the underlying patterns in the data. As a result, it performs poorly on both the training data and unseen test data.

**Characteristics:**

* High training error
* High testing error
* Poor predictive performance

**Causes:**

* Model is too simple
* Insufficient training
* Too few features

**Example:** Using a linear model to fit highly nonlinear data.

**Overfitting** occurs when a model learns not only the underlying patterns but also the **noise and random fluctuations** in the training data. It performs very well on the training data but poorly on new, unseen data.

**Characteristics:**

* Very low training error
* High testing/validation error
* Poor generalization

**Causes:**

* Model is too complex
* Too many features
* Insufficient training data
* Training for too many iterations

**Example:** A very deep decision tree that memorizes the training dataset.

**Difference Between Underfitting and Overfitting**

| Underfitting                                    | Overfitting                                             |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Model is too simple                             | Model is too complex                                    |
| High training error                             | Very low training error                                 |
| High testing error                              | High testing error                                      |
| Fails to learn patterns                         | Learns noise along with patterns                        |
| Poor performance on both training and test data | Good performance on training data but poor on test data |

**Summary:**

* **Underfitting:** The model **does not learn enough** from the data.
* **Overfitting:** The model **learns too much**, including noise, and fails to generalize to new data.

To estimate the relationship between the father's height ((X)) and the son's height ((Y)), we fit the **simple linear regression model**:

- $\beta_0$ = intercept
- $\beta_1$ = slope (regression coefficient)

| Father (X) | 60 | 62 | 64 | 65 | 66 | 67 | 68 | 70 |

$$

### Question 09: K-Means Clustering

<details>

<summary>(a) Describe k-fold Cross Validation</summary>

**k-fold cross-validation** is a model evaluation technique used to estimate how well a machine learning model generalizes to unseen data.

**Steps**

1. Divide the dataset into **k equal-sized subsets (folds)**.
2. Select one fold as the **test (validation) set**.
3. Use the remaining **k−1 folds** as the **training set**.
4. Train the model and evaluate its performance on the test fold.
5. Repeat the process **k times**, using a different fold as the test set each time.
6. Compute the **average performance** (e.g., accuracy, precision, recall) over all k trials.

**Advantages**

* Uses all data for both training and testing.
* Reduces bias caused by a single train-test split.
* Produces a more reliable estimate of model performance.

<summary>(b) Define Core Point, Boundary Point, and Noise Point</summary>

These terms are used in **DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise)**.

1. Core Point: A **core point** is a point that has at least **MinPts** neighboring points within a radius **ε (epsilon)**.
   * Located in a dense region.
   * Used to expand clusters.
2. Boundary (Border) Point: A **boundary (border) point** is a point that:
   * Has fewer than **MinPts** neighbors within ε.
   * Lies within the ε-neighborhood of a **core point**.

   It belongs to a cluster but **cannot expand** the cluster.
3. Noise (Outlier) Point: A **noise point** is a point that:
   * Is neither a core point nor a boundary point.
   * Does not belong to any cluster.
   * Is considered an outlier.
