The **IELTS Academic Speaking test** is a face-to-face interview with an IELTS examiner. It assesses how effectively you can communicate in spoken English in everyday and academic/professional situations.

An important point: **the Speaking test is the same for IELTS Academic and IELTS General Training.** The difference between Academic and General Training is mainly in the Reading and Writing tests.

## Basic Test Structure

![IELTS Speaking](/img/ielts/speaking.png)

### Part 1 vs Part 2 vs Part 3

A useful way to think about the three sections is:

#### Part 1

**Personal → familiar**

> "What do you do in your free time?"

#### Part 2

**Personal experience → extended speech**

> "Describe an activity you enjoy."

#### Part 3

**General/abstract → analysis**

> "Why do people have less free time nowadays?"

So the difficulty generally progresses:

**Personal → Extended → Analytical**

## A Practical Answer Formula

For many questions, you can use:

### Part 1

> Answer → Reason → Example

For example:

**Question:**

> Do you enjoy reading?

**Answer:**

> Yes, I do.

**Reason:**

> I find reading useful because it allows me to learn about subjects that I'm interested in.

**Example:**

> For example, I often read articles about artificial intelligence and computer science in my spare time.

This produces a much stronger response than:

> Yes, I like reading.

### Part 2

**Introduction → Background → Details → Example → Feelings/importance → Conclusion**

> What → When → Where → How → Example → Why → Feeling → Conclusion

### Part 3

**Opinion → Reason → Explanation → Example → Contrast/qualification**

For example:

> I think technology has made education more accessible. This is mainly because students can now access courses and educational materials from almost anywhere. For example, someone living in a rural area can watch lectures online without travelling to a major city. However, access to reliable internet is still a problem in some areas.

## Part 1: Introduction and Interview

### Duration: 4–5 minutes

Part 1 is designed to make you comfortable and assess your ability to communicate about **familiar, everyday topics**.

The examiner will introduce themselves and verify your identity before asking questions.

### Typical topics

You may be asked about:

* Your hometown
* Your studies
* Your work
* Your family
* Your hobbies
* Your free time
* Sports
* Food
* Friends
* Daily routine
* Weather
* Transportation
* Technology
* Books
* Music
* Shopping
* Holidays
* Social media

### Example

**Examiner:**

> Where are you from?

**Candidate:**

> I'm from Jessore, a city in southwestern Bangladesh. It's relatively peaceful, and I particularly like the fact that there are quite a few green areas around the city.

Then the examiner might ask:

> What do you like most about your hometown?

A good response would be more than simply:

> I like the environment.

Instead:

> What I like most is the relatively peaceful environment. Compared with larger cities, it's less crowded, and I can easily find quiet places to relax. I also enjoy spending time with my friends there.

### How long should you answer?

You don't need to give a long speech.

A useful target is approximately **2–4 sentences** for most Part 1 questions.

Think:

**Answer → Reason → Example/detail**

For example:

> **Answer:** Yes, I enjoy reading.
> **Reason:** It helps me relax and also improves my knowledge.
> **Example:** Recently, I've been reading books about artificial intelligence because that's related to my studies.

## Part 2: Individual Long Turn

### Duration: 3–4 minutes total

This is often called the **cue card** section.

The examiner gives you a topic card containing a main topic and several points that you should talk about.

You receive:

* **1 minute to prepare**
* Paper and pencil for notes
* **1–2 minutes to speak**

The examiner will tell you when to start and will stop you after approximately two minutes.

### Example cue card

> **Describe a skill you would like to learn.**
>
> You should say:
>
> * what the skill is
> * why you want to learn it
> * how you would learn it
> * and explain how this skill would be useful to you.

### Your preparation

You have only **one minute**, so don't try to write complete sentences.

Write keywords.

For example:

```text
Skill → public speaking

Why?
→ confidence
→ presentations
→ career

How?
→ YouTube
→ practice
→ university presentations

Useful?
→ communication
→ interviews
→ teamwork
```

Then use those keywords to create a natural response.

### A useful structure

You can organize your answer like this:

**1. Introduction**

> The skill I'd really like to develop is public speaking.

**2. Background**

> I've had some experience giving presentations at university, but I still feel that I need to become more confident when speaking in front of a large audience.

**3. Why you want it**

> The main reason I'd like to improve this skill is that communication is extremely important in both academic and professional life.

**4. How you'll learn**

> I would probably start by watching professional speakers online and then practice regularly with my classmates.

**5. Example**

> For example, I could record myself giving a short presentation and then review my pronunciation, vocabulary, and body language.

**6. Conclusion**

> So, overall, I think public speaking would be a very useful skill for my future career.

## Part 3: Discussion

### Duration: 4–5 minutes

Part 3 is different from Part 1.

The examiner asks **more abstract and analytical questions** related to the topic from Part 2.

You may need to:

* Explain causes
* Discuss advantages/disadvantages
* Compare situations
* Give opinions
* Explain changes
* Discuss society
* Predict possible developments
* Justify arguments
* Give examples

### Example

Suppose Part 2 was about learning a skill.

The examiner might ask:

> Why do you think communication skills are important in modern society?

A stronger answer might be:

> I think communication skills have become increasingly important because people now work with individuals from different backgrounds and often communicate through digital platforms. Good communication can reduce misunderstandings and make teamwork more effective. For example, in an international company, employees may need to communicate with colleagues from several different countries.

Then the examiner could ask:

> Do you think schools should teach communication skills?

You could say:

> Yes, I think they should be included more systematically in education. Academic knowledge is important, but students also need to explain their ideas clearly, participate in discussions, and work effectively with others. These abilities can be particularly useful when they enter university or the workplace.

## Assessment Criteria

Your Speaking performance is evaluated using **four criteria**.

Each criterion contributes equally to your Speaking band score.

### 1. Fluency and Coherence

This measures how effectively you can speak continuously and organize your ideas.

The examiner considers things such as:

* How smoothly you speak
* How often you hesitate
* Whether you can continue speaking
* How logically you organize ideas
* Whether you connect ideas appropriately

#### Lower-level response

> I like technology... um... because... technology is useful... and... I use it every day... um... especially... phones.

The speaker has difficulty maintaining a continuous response.

#### Better response

> I use technology extensively in my daily life, mainly because it makes communication and studying much easier. For example, I regularly use my phone and laptop to communicate with my classmates and to access educational resources.

The ideas flow more naturally.

#### Important

**Fluency does NOT mean speaking extremely fast.**

You should aim for:

> Natural + clear + reasonably continuous

rather than:

> Extremely fast

### 2. Lexical Resource

This means your <span className="danger">**vocabulary**</span>.

The examiner considers:

* Range of vocabulary
* Accuracy
* Ability to choose appropriate words
* Ability to paraphrase
* Ability to discuss different topics

You don't need to use complicated words in every sentence.

You need:

* wide vocabulary
* precise vocabulary
* natural collocations
* ability to paraphrase
* flexibility

For example:

Instead of repeatedly saying:

> very important

you might use:

> highly significant
> particularly important
> essential
> a key factor

depending on context.

#### Basic

> Technology is very good because it helps people communicate.

#### More developed

> Technology has transformed the way people communicate, particularly by making instant communication possible across long distances.

The second response uses vocabulary more precisely.

#### Avoid memorized "big words"

For example, don't force words like:

> ubiquitous
> multifaceted
> quintessential
> ameliorate

into every answer.

Using a sophisticated word incorrectly can hurt rather than help your communication.

**Natural and accurate vocabulary is more important than showing off difficult words.**

### 3. Grammatical Range and Accuracy

The examiner evaluates:

* Grammar accuracy
* Sentence structures
* Variety of structures
* Ability to use complex sentences

#### Simple sentence

> I enjoy reading books.

#### Compound sentence

> I enjoy reading books, and I usually read before going to bed.

#### Complex sentence

> Although I don't have much free time during the semester, I try to read whenever I get the opportunity.

A strong candidate can naturally use a mixture of:

* Simple sentences
* Compound sentences
* Complex sentences
* Conditional sentences
* Relative clauses
* Different tenses

You don't need every sentence to be complex.

### 4. Pronunciation

Pronunciation is about how clearly and effectively you communicate.

The examiner considers:

* Individual sounds
* Word stress
* Sentence stress
* Intonation
* Rhythm
* Connected speech
* Overall intelligibility

You **do not need a British or American accent**.

For example, having a Bangladeshi accent isn't automatically a problem.

The key question is essentially:

> Can the examiner understand you easily?

#### Focus on

**Word stress**

For example:

> PHOtograph
> phoTOGraphy

**Sentence stress**

> I **really** enjoyed the experience.

**Intonation**

Avoid speaking every sentence with exactly the same pitch.

## How You Should Prepare

A practical preparation routine could look like this:

### 1. Build topic vocabulary

Prepare vocabulary for approximately:

* Education
* Technology
* Environment
* Health
* Work
* Hobbies
* Travel
* Food
* Family
* Society
* Transportation
* Media
* Communication

### 2. Practice Part 1

Take a topic and answer **5–10 questions** aloud.

Don't write full answers.

### 3. Practice cue cards

Take a random cue card.

Set a timer:

**1 minute preparation → 2 minutes speaking**

Record yourself.

Then listen and identify:

* Long pauses
* Repeated words
* Grammar mistakes
* Pronunciation problems
* Weak vocabulary

### 4. Practice Part 3

For every Part 2 topic, create broader questions.

For example:

**Part 2:** Describe a technology you use.

Then:

> How has technology changed education?

> Are young people too dependent on technology?

> Will technology replace teachers?

> What problems can technology create?

This trains you to move from **personal experience to abstract discussion**.

### The Most Important Principle

Don't think:

> **"I need to sound like a native speaker."**

Think:

> **"I need to communicate my ideas clearly, naturally, and accurately."**

## Common Mistakes

### ❌ Giving extremely short answers

> Examiner: Do you like music?

> Candidate: Yes.

This doesn't give you enough opportunity to demonstrate your English.

### ❌ Giving memorized speeches

If the examiner asks something slightly different, a memorized answer can become unnatural and difficult to adapt.

### ❌ Speaking too quickly

Fast speech doesn't automatically mean fluent speech.

### ❌ Using difficult vocabulary unnecessarily

Accuracy and natural usage are more important.

### ❌ Repeating the same words

Instead of repeatedly saying:

> good, good, good...

you can use:

> useful
> enjoyable
> beneficial
> effective
> interesting
> valuable

—but only when they genuinely fit the context.

### ❌ Ignoring the question

Make sure your answer directly addresses what the examiner asked.

## Questions

<details>

<summary>Should You Memorize Answers?</summary>

I strongly recommend **not memorizing complete answers**.

Instead, prepare:

* Useful vocabulary
* Common topics
* Personal examples
* Ideas
* Sentence structures
* Linking expressions

For example, learn flexible expressions such as:

> One of the main reasons is...

> Another factor is...

> From my perspective...

> For example...

> Having said that...

> Compared with the past...

> This is particularly important because...

Then adapt them to the question.

</details>

<details>

<summary>What Happens If You Don't Understand the Question?</summary>

You can ask the examiner to repeat it.

For example:

> Sorry, could you repeat the question, please?

Or:

> Could you explain what you mean by that?

This is much better than answering a completely different question.

</details>

<details>

<summary>What If You Make a Mistake?</summary>

**Don't panic.**

Simply correct yourself and continue.

For example:

> I went there last year—sorry, I mean two years ago—and I really enjoyed the experience.

One mistake does not destroy your score.

The examiner evaluates your **overall performance**.

</details>

<details>

<summary>What If You Have No Idea About the Topic?</summary>

You don't need expert knowledge.

IELTS Speaking is primarily testing your **English communication**, not your knowledge of the world.

You can say:

> That's an interesting question. I've never really thought about it before, but I'd say...

Then develop a reasonable answer.

For example:

> I've never really thought about that before, but I'd say people probably spend less time talking to their neighbors nowadays because they have busier lifestyles and can communicate with friends online.

You don't need specialist knowledge.

</details>
